# Slide 7 — Routing + Traffic Engineering

This slide is **very important for your professor Q&A**, because it explains a key design decision in B4:

> **B4 did not throw away traditional routing. It layered Traffic Engineering on top of it.**

The paper explicitly says B4 kept existing routing protocols and deployed centralized TE as an overlay. 

---

## 1. What problem are we solving?

Remember our distinction:

**Routing:**

> “Which paths are available?”

**Traffic Engineering (TE):**

> “How much traffic should use each available path?”

For example:

```text
             ┌─── B ───┐
             │          │
Data Center A           D
             │          │
             └─── C ───┘
```

Suppose A needs to send **100 Gbps** to D.

There are two possible paths:

```text
A → B → D
A → C → D
```

Traditional routing can determine paths toward D.

But B4 wants to do something more sophisticated:

```text
A → B → D     60 Gbps
A → C → D     40 Gbps
```

So the question becomes:

> **How do we distribute traffic across multiple paths efficiently?**

That's where centralized TE comes in.

---

# 2. The important B4 design

Put this on your slide:

```text
                 B4 CONTROL
                     │
          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
          │                     │
     Traditional             Centralized
      Routing                    TE
          │                     │
      BGP / IS-IS          Traffic allocation
          │                     │
          └──────────┬──────────┘
                     │
                     ▼
              Switch Forwarding
```

The important idea is:

**Routing provides the baseline network behavior.**

**TE modifies/augments forwarding to distribute traffic across paths.**

The paper specifically describes routing and TE as **independent services**, with centralized TE deployed as an overlay on top of standard routing. 

---

# 3. Why didn't Google replace routing completely?

This is a **very likely professor question**.

You might think:

> “If B4 has a centralized controller, why not just replace BGP/IS-IS with the centralized controller?”

The paper gives several reasons.

### Reason 1 — Gradual deployment

Google already had a functioning routing system.

Replacing everything at once would be risky.

Instead:

```text
Existing routing
       ↓
Keep it working
       ↓
Add SDN infrastructure
       ↓
Add centralized TE
```

This allowed B4 to introduce SDN gradually. 

### Reason 2 — Interoperability

B4 needed to coexist with parts of Google's network that still used traditional routing.

So keeping standard routing protocols made integration easier.

### Reason 3 — Safety

This is one of the **best points to remember for your seminar**.

Because TE was layered on top of baseline routing, Google could disable TE if something went wrong.

Then:

```text
TE enabled
    ↓
Centralized traffic engineering
    ↓
Something goes wrong
    ↓
Disable TE
    ↓
Fall back to shortest-path forwarding
```

The paper calls this the **“big red button.”** 

That's actually a very good engineering principle:

> **Introduce a new system without making the old system your only fallback.**

# 4. What does the forwarding decision look like?

Conceptually:

```text
                 PACKET
                   │
                   ▼
          ┌─────────────────┐
          │ Does TE rule    │
          │ match?          │
          └───────┬─────────┘
                  │
            ┌─────┴─────┐
            │           │
           YES          NO
            │           │
            ▼           ▼
       Use TE paths   Normal routing
       + weights      / shortest path
            │           │
            └─────┬─────┘
                  ▼
               Forward
```

So B4 isn't saying:

> “Traditional routing is useless.”

Instead, it's saying:

> “Traditional routing gives us a baseline, and centralized TE gives us more control over how traffic is distributed.”

That's a much more accurate way to explain B4.

# 5. One subtle point that can impress your professor

B4's topology abstraction is interesting.

The physical network may look like:

```text
             multiple physical links
          ┌───────┬───────┬───────┐
          │       │       │       │
       Site A  ────────────────  Site B
```

But the TE system doesn't need to reason about every physical link individually.

It can think of:

```text
        Site A ───────── Site B
             100 Gbps
```

So:

> **TE abstracts each site as a logical node and site-to-site connectivity as logical edges.**

B4 then uses a custom version of ECMP hashing to distribute traffic across the physical links belonging to that logical connection. 

This helps the centralized TE system scale.

Don't memorize every word. Understand the story.

“An important design decision in B4 was that Google did not completely replace traditional routing.

B4 continued to use standard routing protocols such as BGP and IS-IS to provide baseline routing. On top of this, Google deployed centralized Traffic Engineering.

The reason is that routing and Traffic Engineering solve different problems. Routing determines available paths, while Traffic Engineering decides how traffic should be distributed across those paths.

This layered design also provided an important safety mechanism. If there was a problem with Traffic Engineering, Google could disable TE and fall back to shortest-path forwarding. The paper refers to this as the ‘big red button.’

Therefore, B4 was not simply a replacement for traditional routing. It was a hybrid architecture combining existing routing with centralized SDN-based Traffic Engineering.”

# 7. Professor Q&A

### Q: Why isn't normal routing enough for B4?

**Answer:**

Normal routing can select paths, but B4 needs to **distribute traffic across multiple paths according to bandwidth demand and application priorities**. That requires Traffic Engineering.

---

### Q: Did B4 completely replace BGP and IS-IS?

**Answer:**

**No.** B4 retained existing routing protocols and layered centralized Traffic Engineering on top of them. 

---

### Q: Why is this hybrid approach useful?

**Answer:**

It allowed Google to introduce SDN gradually, maintain interoperability with existing routing, and retain a fallback to normal shortest-path forwarding if TE failed. 

---

### Q: What is the “big red button”?

**Answer:**

It's the ability to **disable Traffic Engineering and fall back to shortest-path forwarding** if the TE system encounters a critical problem. 

---

### Q: Does centralized TE mean routing is also centralized?

**Answer:**

Not necessarily. In B4, **routing and TE were separate services**. Traditional routing continued to provide baseline routing, while centralized TE operated as an overlay.
