# 1. What are they comparing?

The baseline is:

### Shortest-path routing

Imagine:

```text
A ── B ── D
     ↑
   shortest

A ── C ── D
     ↑
   alternate
```

Suppose the shortest path is:

```text
A → B → D
```

Normally, traffic would primarily follow that path.

But perhaps:

```text
A → B → D = heavily loaded

A → C → D = plenty of capacity
```

Shortest-path routing doesn't necessarily exploit the alternate path in the way B4's centralized TE can.

B4 says:

> "Why not distribute traffic across multiple available paths?"

So:

```text
                 B
               ↗
A ────────────
               ↘
                 C
                  ↓
                  D
```

Traffic can be split across paths.

---

# 2. What does "throughput improvement" mean?

Very simply:

### Throughput = how much useful traffic the network can carry.

If a network can effectively carry:

```text
100 units
```

and another configuration can carry:

```text
114 units
```

then that's approximately:

**14% improvement.**

The paper reports:

> **14% average throughput increase**

for B4's TE compared with the shortest-path baseline. 

This is the number I want you to remember.

---

# 3. Why do more paths help?

Suppose you only allow one path:

```text
A → B → D
```

You have limited flexibility.

Now allow two:

```text
A → B → D
A → C → D
```

Better.

Now three:

```text
A → B → D
A → C → D
A → E → D
```

Even more flexibility.

So generally:

```text
More paths
     ↓
More choices
     ↓
Better traffic distribution
     ↓
Higher throughput
```

But there's a catch.

---

# 4. More paths eventually stop helping much

The paper found that throughput improvement **flattens at around 4 paths**.

Meaning:

```text
1 path   → improvement
2 paths  → more improvement
3 paths  → more improvement
4 paths  → strong improvement

5 paths  → small additional benefit
6 paths  → small additional benefit
...
```

Why not just use 20 paths?

Because more paths require more **hardware table resources** and more complexity.

The paper explicitly points out this tradeoff: more paths give TE more flexibility, but consume additional hardware table resources. 

So Google chose:

> **4 paths**

for the production deployment.

---

# 5. What is the "split quantum"?

This is something your professor **could definitely ask**.

We previously discussed traffic splitting:

```text
T1 = 60%
T2 = 40%
```

But actual switch hardware doesn't necessarily allow completely arbitrary ratios.

So B4 has a concept called:

### Path-split quantum

Think of it as the **smallest adjustment step** allowed when choosing traffic split ratios.

For example, conceptually:

```text
Quantum = 1/4

Possible split steps:
0
25%
50%
75%
100%
```

The paper evaluates finer and coarser split granularities.

---

# 6. What did they discover about split granularity?

They found:

> Throughput improves as splits become finer, but the improvement flattens around **1/16**.

So:

```text
Coarse splitting
      ↓
Less flexibility

Finer splitting
      ↓
More flexibility

Around 1/16
      ↓
Benefit begins to flatten
```

But here's the interesting part:

### Production didn't use 1/16.

They chose:

> **1/4 quantum + 4 paths**

Why?

Because the finer split provides more flexibility but also consumes additional hardware table resources.

So B4 deliberately chose a **practical engineering tradeoff**, not simply the mathematically best configuration.

# 8. But here's an important subtle point

The paper says the **main benefits are particularly important during high demand or failures**.

The 14% is the average result.

Suppose a high-priority data copy happens:

```text

      ↓
```

If you move that traffic onto an alternate path, the paper says the average utilization improvement over the whole week is only about:

That might sound small.

### It can reduce the required deployed WAN capacity by a factor of 2.

# 9. Why is that important?

Imagine Google needs:

```text
200 Gbps
```

of deployed capacity if traffic follows the shortest path.

With better TE, perhaps the same workload can be handled with roughly:

```text
100 Gbps
```

in the scenario described by the paper.

The paper's point is not simply:

> "We increased average throughput."

The bigger engineering benefit can be:

> **We can avoid buying/provisioning as much extra network capacity.**

That's a much more powerful business argument.

# 10. B4 pushes utilization very high

Now remember Slide 2.

Traditional WAN:

```text
Typical target:
30–40% utilization
```

Why?

Because they want spare capacity for failures.

B4 takes a different approach.

The paper reports that:

> **B4's busiest edges ran near 100% utilization, and almost all links reached full utilization during the course of the day.**



That sounds dangerous at first.

So the professor may ask:

> "If B4 uses almost 100% of the network, what happens during failures?"

Excellent question.

And that's exactly why B4's traffic priorities matter.

---

# 11. How can B4 safely use high utilization?

B4 differentiates traffic classes.

Think:

```text
              Network capacity
                    │
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          │                   │
    High priority        Low priority
          │                   │
     protect it        can tolerate loss
                          / throttling
```

Suppose a link becomes heavily loaded.

B4 tries to ensure that:

**high-priority traffic remains protected.**

Low-priority traffic can be throttled or tolerate loss.

The paper specifically describes using substantial low-priority traffic on highly utilized edges so local QoS mechanisms can protect high-priority traffic. 

This is one reason B4's **application awareness** is important.
