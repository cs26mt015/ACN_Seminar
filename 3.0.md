# Slide 4 — Problem with traditional routing

Now make the problem concrete.

Suppose:

```text
             Path 1
        ┌──────────────┐
        │              │
A ──────┤              ├────── B
        │              │
        └──── Path 2 ──┘
```

Traditional shortest-path routing might prefer:

```text
Path 1
```

even if:

```text
Path 1 = congested
Path 2 = mostly unused
```

So:

```text
Shortest path
      ↓
Traffic concentration
      ↓
Congestion
      ↓
Unused capacity elsewhere
```

### Key message:

> **Routing finds paths; it doesn't necessarily optimize how traffic should be distributed across all available paths.**

This sentence is extremely useful throughout your seminar.

# Part 5 — What NOT to do

Because this is your first seminar, these are worth knowing.

### ❌ Don't put full paragraphs on slides

Bad:

```text
B4 uses centralized traffic engineering to allocate
bandwidth among competing applications and uses...
```

Good:

```text
B4 TE

• Centralized control
• Multiple paths
• Fair allocation
• High utilization
```

---

### ❌ Don't put every equation from the paper

You don't need to reproduce every mathematical detail unless your professor specifically expects mathematical analysis.

For your seminar, understanding the algorithm is more important than filling slides with equations.

---

### ❌ Don't explain every implementation detail

You don't need to explain every line of the paper.

Your job is to communicate:

> **problem → idea → mechanism → evidence → lessons**

---

### ❌ Don't hide limitations

If the professor asks:

> "Are there any disadvantages?"

and you say:

> "No, B4 is very efficient."

That's a weak answer.

Instead:

> "The production experience showed that switch programming latency and control-plane scalability could become bottlenecks, and the documented outage showed that operational and control-plane failures required careful handling."  

That sounds like someone who actually studied the paper.

# Part 6 — Your professor's likely Q&A

Now that you've learned the paper, we can predict the questions.

### Basic

**Q1. What is B4?**

> Google's software-defined private WAN for connecting data centers and efficiently utilizing WAN bandwidth.

**Q2. Why was B4 needed?**

> Traditional WANs were provisioned conservatively, leaving significant bandwidth unused, while Google's internal traffic was large and largely elastic.

**Q3. What is SDN?**

> A networking approach where control decisions are separated from packet forwarding and can be managed programmatically.

---

### Architecture

**Q4. What are the three B4 layers?**

> Global layer, site controller layer, and switch hardware layer. 

**Q5. Does B4 replace BGP and IS-IS?**

> No. B4 retains traditional routing and layers centralized TE on top of it. 

---

### Technical

**Q6. What is a Flow Group?**

> A source site, destination site, and QoS combination used as a unit for traffic engineering. 

**Q7. What is a Tunnel Group?**

> A set of tunnels associated with a Flow Group, with weights specifying the fraction of traffic assigned to each tunnel. 

**Q8. Why use multiple tunnels?**

> To distribute traffic across multiple paths and improve utilization rather than concentrating traffic on a single shortest path.

**Q9. Why use OpenFlow?**

> To program switch forwarding behavior so that centralized TE decisions can be enforced in the data plane. 

---

### Research-level

**Q10. Why not use an exact LP solution?**

> It was not sufficiently scalable; B4 used a faster algorithm that achieved similar fairness and at least 99% bandwidth utilization while being about 25× faster in the deployment evaluation. 

**Q11. What was the main performance improvement?**

> 14% average throughput improvement compared with shortest-path routing. 

**Q12. What was an important limitation discovered in production?**

> Switch programming and control-plane behavior could become significant performance bottlenecks, as demonstrated by the production outage and TE operation measurements.
