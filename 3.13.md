# Slide 13 — B4 in Production: Did It Actually Work?

Now we move from **design** to **real-world deployment**.

This is one of the strongest parts of your seminar because the paper is not just proposing B4 theoretically.

Google actually deployed B4 in its production WAN and collected operational experience from it. 

---

## 1. Why production deployment matters

Imagine a research paper saying:

> "Our algorithm works in simulation."

That's useful, but there can still be problems when deployed.

B4 went much further:

```text
Research idea
     ↓
Implementation
     ↓
Google production WAN
     ↓
Real traffic
     ↓
Real failures
     ↓
Operational lessons
```

That's why the paper is called:

> **Experience with a Globally-Deployed Software Defined WAN**

The word **"Experience"** is important.

Google is telling us what happened when this system was actually operated.

---

# 2. B4's scale

During the September–November 2012 measurement period, the paper reports:

| Metric                       |     Value |
| ---------------------------- | --------: |
| Sites                        |    **16** |
| Unidirectional edges         |    **46** |
| Tunnel Groups                |   **240** |
| Flow Groups                  | **2,700** |
| Tunnels in use               |   **350** |
| Cached tunnels               | **1,150** |
| Topology changes/day         |   **286** |
| Edge additions/deletions/day |     **7** |



Don't try to memorize every number.

For your seminar, the most useful ones are:

> **16 sites, 46 edges, 2,700 Flow Groups, and 350 tunnels in use.**

These numbers demonstrate that B4 was operating on a meaningful production network rather than a tiny laboratory experiment.

---

# 3. Why were there so many Flow Groups?

Remember what a Flow Group means:

```text
FG = {Source site, Destination site, QoS}
```

So B4 doesn't necessarily treat every individual packet as a separate optimization problem.

Instead, traffic is **aggregated into Flow Groups**.

For example:

```text
Site A → Site D → High QoS
```

can represent a group of related traffic.

That's important for scalability.

Instead of:

```text
Millions of individual packets
        ↓
Millions of decisions
```

B4 works at a higher logical level:

```text
Applications
     ↓
Flow Groups
     ↓
TE decisions
```

---

# 4. B4 had multiple TE servers

Another important production detail:

The paper reports **five geographically distributed TE servers**.

Why not one?

Because a single machine would create a serious failure dependency.

Instead:

```text
             TE Servers
        ┌──────┬──────┬──────┐
        │      │      │      │
       TE1    TE2    TE3   ...
        │      │      │
        └──────┴──────┴──────┘
                 │
          replicated state
```

One server acts as the primary/master while others are available as **hot standbys**.

The paper reports that hot standbys could assume mastership in **less than 10 seconds**. 

---

# 5. What does "hot standby" mean?

Simple example:

```text
             TE Server 1
              MASTER
                 │
                 │
        ┌────────┴────────┐
        ↓                 ↓
     Standby 2         Standby 3
```

The standby servers are already running and prepared to take over.

If the master fails:

```text
Master fails
     ↓
Standby detects failure
     ↓
Standby becomes master
```

This is much faster than:

> "Oh, the server failed. Let's start a new machine and configure everything."

---

# 6. How stable was the system?

The paper reports that the master was stable for an average of about **11 days** before a mastership change. 

That gives us an idea that master changes were not happening constantly.

But here's an important lesson:

> **A production system isn't judged only by whether the main algorithm works.**

You also have to consider:

* failures
* controller delays
* switch programming
* topology changes
* recovery
* operational mistakes

And that's exactly where the next sections of the paper become interesting.

---

# 7. How fast was the TE algorithm?

This is another excellent production result.

The paper reports approximately:

```text
540 TE algorithm runs/day

Average runtime ≈ 0.3 seconds

Maximum runtime ≈ 0.8 seconds
```



That's important because the network was changing frequently.

Remember:

```text
Topology changes
      ↓
Network state changes
      ↓
TE needs to recalculate
      ↓
New Tunnel Groups
      ↓
Switches updated
```

If the optimization took many minutes every time, that would be a problem.

Instead, the actual TE computation was relatively fast.

---

# 8. But here's the surprising part

The **TE algorithm itself wasn't necessarily the biggest bottleneck**.

The paper found substantial latency in **programming the switches**.

This is an important engineering lesson.

Think about the complete process:

```text
Calculate decision
       ↓
Send decision
       ↓
Program switch
       ↓
Switch accepts change
```

The first step might be fast.

But the third step can still be slow.

The paper reports that switch programming latency could be significant, and that existing OpenFlow/software/SDK implementations were not optimized for dynamic forwarding-table programming. 

So:

> **Fast algorithm ≠ fast entire control system.**

That's a great point to mention during your seminar.
