## Slide 16 — A Real B4 Production Outage

This is one of the **most important slides for scoring well**, because now you're not just explaining how B4 was designed—you are showing what happened when the system faced a real production problem.

The paper says B4 had very good availability overall, but it experienced **one substantial outage**, and that outage taught the authors several important lessons about SDN and WAN operation. 

### 1. Start with the big picture

Put this on the slide:

```text
Planned maintenance
        ↓
Duplicate switch ID
        ↓
Link flapping
        ↓
IS-IS LSP flooding
        ↓
OFC ↔ OFA queue overload
        ↓
Queue > 400 MB
        ↓
IS-IS Hello messages delayed
        ↓
Interfaces declared DOWN
        ↓
BGP adjacencies break
        ↓
Existing TE traffic continues
BUT new tunnels cannot be created
        ↓
Operators drain site
        ↓
Disable TE
        ↓
Restart OFCs
        ↓
Recovery
```

This is much better than putting a paragraph on the slide.

---

# 2. What actually happened?

### Step 1 — Planned maintenance

Google was moving about half of the switching hardware at its largest B4 site.

During this maintenance, **one new switch was accidentally configured with the same ID as an existing switch**. 

So:

```text
Switch A → ID = 100
Switch B → ID = 100   ← mistake
```

That created confusion in the routing system.

---

### Step 2 — Link flapping

The duplicate IDs caused **substantial link flapping**.

Link flapping means a network link repeatedly appears to go:

```text
UP → DOWN → UP → DOWN → ...
```

The switches started receiving IS-IS Link State Packets (LSPs) with the same ID but different adjacency information.

They then flooded new LSPs through their interfaces.

So the problem started generating **more and more routing-protocol traffic**. 

---

### Step 3 — The control path became overloaded

Remember our earlier architecture:

```text
Quagga
   ↓
RAP
   ↓
OFC
   ↓
OFA
   ↓
Switch
```

B4's routing-protocol packets were going through this software/control path.

The paper says the **OFC-to-OFA connection was the most constrained part** of their implementation.

The protocol packet queue grew to **more than 400 MB** at its peak. 

This is a VERY important point.

### Why?

Because we previously learned:

> B4's data plane can forward packets very efficiently.

But here the **control path** became the bottleneck.

---

# 3. Why did BGP eventually break?

This is a great professor question.

The queue was full of LSP traffic.

Important IS-IS Hello messages were stuck behind those LSPs.

Eventually the Hello messages were delayed **past their useful lifetime**.

The switches therefore concluded:

> "This interface is no longer alive."

So they declared interfaces down.

That caused **BGP adjacencies with remote sites to break**. 

The chain is:

```text
Too many LSPs
      ↓
Queue becomes huge
      ↓
IS-IS Hellos delayed
      ↓
Interface considered DOWN
      ↓
BGP adjacency breaks
```

### Remember this distinction

**IS-IS** was involved in distributing topology information.

**BGP** adjacencies were subsequently broken because interfaces were declared down.

Don't say:

> "BGP caused the outage."

That's incorrect.

---

# 4. Did all B4 traffic stop?

This is where the paper gets really interesting.

**No.**

Existing TE traffic passing through the site continued to work because the switches **maintained their last known TE state**. 

Think:

```text
Existing TE tunnel
       ↓
Switch already knows how to forward it
       ↓
Continues forwarding
```

But:

```text
NEW tunnel required
       ↓
TE server needs to configure it
       ↓
Control path is broken/constrained
       ↓
Cannot create new tunnel
```

So B4 essentially had:

**Existing traffic → still working**

**New tunnel creation → broken**

That's a very good point to mention during your seminar.

---

# 5. Why was this dangerous?

Suppose an existing TE tunnel was already using a particular path.

That path might later experience another physical failure.

Normally, B4 would want to create a new configuration/tunnel.

But the TE server couldn't create new tunnels through the affected site.

The paper explicitly notes that **concurrent physical failures could therefore leave the network using old broken tunnels**. 

So the problem wasn't simply:

> "Some traffic stopped."

The deeper problem was:

> **The network had lost some of its ability to adapt to new conditions.**

That's an excellent sentence for your presentation.

---

# 6. What did the operators do?

The paper says the operators initially responded by rebooting the servers hosting the OFCs.

Unfortunately, the high system load exposed a **latent OFC bug**, which prevented recovery while the system was under high background load. 

Eventually, they recovered the system by:

```text
Drain the entire site
        ↓
Disable TE
        ↓
Restart OFCs from scratch
        ↓
Recovery
```

So notice something we've already learned:

### The "big red button"

```text
Normal B4 operation
       ↓
Centralized TE
       ↓
Problem
       ↓
Disable TE
       ↓
Fall back to baseline routing
```

That fallback mechanism was important operationally.

---

# 7. What did Google learn?

This is where you can earn extra marks.

The authors extracted several lessons from this outage.

### Lesson 1 — OFC ↔ OFA communication is critical

The paper says the scalability and latency of the packet I/O path between the **OFC and OFA** is critical. 

In simple terms:

> It isn't enough for the TE algorithm to be fast. The system that communicates the decisions to the switches also has to scale.

---

### Lesson 2 — OFA needs more parallelism

The authors suggested making the OFA **asynchronous and multi-threaded**, particularly where multiple switch chips need to be programmed in parallel. 

---

### Lesson 3 — Test systems until they break

The outage happened at their largest B4 site, which was closest to its scalability limits.

The authors say systems should be measured up to their **breaking point**, and rare operations such as OFC recovery should also be tested under stress. 

This is a very strong engineering lesson:

> **A system isn't truly production-ready just because normal operation works. You must test failure and recovery paths too.**

---

### Lesson 4 — Control failure doesn't necessarily invalidate forwarding state

This is subtle but important.

B4 deliberately did not automatically reroute existing traffic when an OFC became unresponsive.

The paper describes this as **"fail open."** 

Meaning:

```text
OFC fails
   ↓
Don't immediately destroy existing forwarding state
   ↓
Existing traffic may continue
```

This can preserve useful traffic, but it also creates a trade-off: the system may not immediately distinguish between a control-plane problem and an actual switch hardware problem.

---

# 8. The most important lesson

If your professor asks:

> **"What is the main lesson from the outage?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"B4 showed that centralized traffic engineering can work at production scale, but the control path itself becomes critical. The TE algorithm may be fast, yet communication between the controller and switches, switch programming, failure recovery, and operational complexity can still become bottlenecks."**

That's a **seminar-level answer** rather than a memorized-paper answer.

---

# 9. How to present Slide 16

You can say:

> "The most interesting part of the paper, in my opinion, is the production outage. During a planned maintenance operation, one new switch was accidentally configured with the same ID as an existing switch. This caused link flapping and large amounts of IS-IS LSP flooding. Because routing-protocol packets passed through the OFC and OFA, the OFC-to-OFA queue grew beyond 400 MB. IS-IS Hello messages were delayed, interfaces were declared down, and BGP adjacencies broke. Interestingly, existing TE traffic continued because the switches retained their last known TE state, but the TE server could no longer create new tunnels through the site. The operators eventually drained the site, disabled TE, and restarted the OFCs. The outage taught the authors that the controller-to-switch communication path, scalability, failure recovery, and operational simplicity are just as important as the TE algorithm itself."

That is a **very strong 60–90 second explanation**.

## Professor Q&A you should know

**Q: What caused the outage?**
A: A switch was accidentally configured with the same ID as an existing switch during planned maintenance, causing link flapping and excessive IS-IS LSP processing.

**Q: Why did BGP adjacencies break?**
A: IS-IS Hello messages were delayed behind queued LSPs, causing switches to declare interfaces down, which broke BGP adjacencies.

**Q: Did all traffic stop?**
A: No. Existing TE transit traffic continued using the last known TE state, but new tunnels could not be created through the affected site.

**Q: What was the biggest technical bottleneck?**
A: The OFC-to-OFA communication path became heavily congested; its queue exceeded 400 MB.

**Q: What did the operators finally do?**
A: They drained the site, disabled TE, and restarted the OFCs from scratch.

**Q: Was B4 considered a failure?**
A: No. The outage exposed weaknesses in a real production system and gave the authors important engineering lessons about scalability, control paths, failure recovery, and operations.

**Q: Why is this slide important?**
A: Because it demonstrates the difference between a system that works in normal conditions and a system that can handle real-world failures.

---

### 🔑 One sentence to remember

> **B4's biggest lesson was that optimizing the network is only half the problem—the control system that manages the network must also survive scale, failures, and human mistakes.**

Once this makes sense, **Slide 17 is the final slide: Conclusion — what B4 actually proved.**
