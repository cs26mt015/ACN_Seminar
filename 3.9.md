## Slide 9 — How Does B4 Decide the Traffic Split?

Now we get to an **important technical part of the paper**.

So far we know:

> **Flow Group → multiple Tunnels → Tunnel Group → percentage of traffic on each tunnel**

But the obvious question is:

### **Who decides whether it should be 50/50, 60/40, 80/20, etc.?**

That's what the **Traffic Engineering (TE) algorithm** does.

---

# 1. What is B4 trying to achieve?

Imagine two applications are sending traffic:

```text
             Link capacity
                  ↓
        ┌──────────────────┐
A ─────→│                  │─────→ D
        │   Network        │
B ─────→│                  │
        └──────────────────┘
```

Suppose several applications want bandwidth at the same time.

B4 wants to allocate bandwidth **fairly**, while also respecting application priorities and available network capacity.

The paper describes the objective as **max-min fairness**.

### Simple meaning of max-min fairness

Give everyone a reasonable amount first.

Then:

> If there is extra bandwidth, give more to applications that can use it, without unfairly starving others.

Think of it like distributing food:

```text
10 people
100 pieces of food

First:
Everyone gets a reasonable share.

Then:
Remaining food is distributed without
letting one person take almost everything.
```

The exact optimization is more complicated than this analogy, but this is the mental model you should remember.

---

# 2. Why isn't every tunnel simply 50/50?

This is a very likely professor question.

Suppose:

```text
A ─── B ─── D
 \          /
  \── C ───/
```

There are two possible tunnels:

```text
Tunnel 1: A → B → D
Tunnel 2: A → C → D
```

You might think:

```text
50% → Tunnel 1
50% → Tunnel 2
```

But what if Tunnel 1 has much less available capacity?

Then 50/50 could overload it.

So B4 needs to consider **network capacity and competing traffic**.

For example:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 30%
Tunnel 2 → 70%
```

might be better than:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 50%
Tunnel 2 → 50%
```

The percentages are therefore **computed by the TE system**, not chosen randomly.

The paper's Tunnel Groups represent these weighted allocations. 

---

# 3. B4 also considers application priorities

This is another important part.

Not all traffic necessarily has the same importance.

For example:

```text
High priority:
Database replication

Medium priority:
Index update

Low priority:
Large background copy
```

B4 uses **bandwidth functions** to express how bandwidth should be allocated to applications, including priorities.

The paper gives an example where applications can have different weights such as:

```text
Application 1 → weight 10
Application 2 → weight 1
Application 3 → weight 0.5
```

So the TE system isn't simply saying:

> "Everybody gets the same bandwidth."

Instead, the allocation can reflect the application's desired priority. 

---

# 4. The important chain

You should memorize this:

```text
Applications
     ↓
Bandwidth requirements / priorities
     ↓
TE optimization
     ↓
Choose tunnels
     ↓
Calculate traffic split
     ↓
Tunnel Group
     ↓
OpenFlow
     ↓
Switches
```

For example:

```text
FG: A → D

Tunnel 1: A → B → D     30%
Tunnel 2: A → C → D     70%
```

That **30/70** becomes part of the Tunnel Group.

---

# 5. But there is a problem...

Now we reach one of the more interesting engineering decisions in the paper.

A mathematically exact optimization problem can be expensive to solve.

The paper describes the ideal allocation as an optimization problem, but solving the exact **linear program (LP)** repeatedly was not scalable enough for B4's operational needs.

So Google designed a faster algorithm called:

### **Tunnel Group Generation and Quantization**

Don't worry about the name yet.

We'll break it down in the next slide.

The important idea for **this slide** is:

> B4 wants an allocation that is close to the ideal fair allocation, but it also needs to calculate that allocation quickly enough for a production network.

The paper reports that the B4 algorithm achieved similar fairness while being about **25× faster than the LP approach**, with at least **99% bandwidth utilization** in their evaluation.

# 🧠 Professor Questions

### Q1. Why doesn't B4 simply split traffic equally?

**Answer:**

> Because the available capacity and competing traffic can be different on different paths. Equal splitting can cause congestion or inefficient utilization.

---

### Q2. What is max-min fairness?

**Answer:**

> It is a fairness objective that tries to maximize the allocation of the least-served traffic demands before giving additional bandwidth to demands that can receive more.

---

### Q3. Does B4 give every application the same bandwidth?

**Answer:**

> No. B4 uses bandwidth functions that can represent different application priorities and desired allocations.

---

### Q4. Why not always use the exact linear-program solution?

**Answer:**

> Because B4 operates in a production network and needs the optimization to be computationally efficient. The paper reports that its algorithm was about 25× faster than the LP approach while achieving similar fairness.

---

### Q5. Who actually calculates the 30/70 split?

**Answer:**

> The centralized **Traffic Engineering Server** calculates the traffic allocation and produces the corresponding Tunnel Groups.
