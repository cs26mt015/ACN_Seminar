# Slide 14 — B4 Performance Results

Now we answer the big question:

> **Did B4's traffic engineering actually improve network performance?**

This is one of the most important slides because it gives us **evidence**, not just architecture.

The paper compares B4's TE behavior against **shortest-path routing** and studies how the number of available paths and traffic-splitting granularity affect throughput. 

---

## 1. First comparison: Shortest Path vs B4 TE

Remember our earlier example:

```text
              B
             / \
A ──────────    ───────── D
             \ /
              C
```

Traditional shortest-path routing might prefer one path:

```text
A → B → D
```

Even if:

```text
A → B → D = heavily loaded
A → C → D = lightly loaded
```

B4 can use both:

```text
A → B → D   40%
A → C → D   60%
```

So instead of asking:

> "What is the shortest path?"

B4 asks:

> **"How can I use the available network capacity more effectively?"**

---

# 2. More paths help — but only up to a point

The paper evaluates how throughput changes as B4 uses more paths.

The interesting result is:

> **Throughput improvement begins to flatten around 4 paths.** 

Conceptually:

```text
Throughput
   │
   │                 ________
   │             ___/
   │          __/
   │       __/
   │______/ 
   └──────────────────────────
       1   2   3   4   5   6
             Number of paths
```

The exact shape above is just a conceptual illustration.

The important observation is:

```text
More paths
    ↓
More opportunity to use capacity
    ↓
Improvement
    ↓
Eventually diminishing returns
```

So B4 didn't need an enormous number of paths to get most of the benefit.

---

# 3. What about finer traffic splits?

Suppose we have:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 50%
Tunnel 2 → 50%
```

That's relatively coarse.

But perhaps the ideal allocation is:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 53%
Tunnel 2 → 47%
```

If the system allows finer-grained ratios, it can get closer to the ideal allocation.

The paper studies this using different **quantization levels**.

It found that throughput improved as the traffic-splitting granularity became finer, with improvements leveling off around a **1/16 quantum**. 

You don't need to memorize the mathematics behind "quantum."

Just remember:

> **Smaller quantum → finer traffic splits → closer to the ideal allocation.**

---

# 4. What did B4 actually deploy?

Here's the practical engineering decision.

Even though finer splitting could provide additional improvement, Google didn't necessarily choose the theoretically finest possible configuration.

The deployment used:

```text
Quantum = 1/4

Paths = 4
```



Why?

Because production systems have trade-offs.

You don't want to maximize one metric while making the system unnecessarily complicated or expensive.

This is an excellent research-paper lesson:

> **The best theoretical configuration isn't always the best production configuration.**

---

# 5. The headline result: 14% average throughput improvement

This is the number I definitely want you to remember.

The paper reports:

### **14% average throughput improvement**

compared with shortest-path routing under the evaluated conditions. 

So:

```text
Shortest-path baseline
        │
        │
        ↓
      B4 TE
        │
        ↓
 ~14% average throughput improvement
```

This is one of the strongest numbers to put on your PPT.

---

# 6. Another important result: capacity savings

The paper also describes a practical example involving a **weekly high-priority copy**.

By moving that traffic away from the shortest path, B4 achieved approximately:

> **5% improvement in weekly utilization**

while potentially **halving the required deployed capacity**. 

This is an important distinction.

### Throughput improvement

Means:

> We can move traffic more effectively.

### Capacity savings

Means:

> We may not need to buy/deploy as much network capacity.

For Google, the second one can be extremely valuable because WAN capacity and high-end networking equipment are expensive.

---

# 7. Network utilization

The paper also reports that the busiest edges reached close to **100% utilization**, and almost all links were fully utilized during the day. 

That might sound strange.

Earlier we said traditional WANs intentionally operated around 30–40% average utilization.

Now B4 is pushing utilization much higher.

Why is that possible?

Because B4 has better visibility and control over:

* traffic
* applications
* bandwidth allocation
* priorities
* paths

And much of the traffic is **elastic**, meaning it can tolerate changes in available bandwidth.

So B4 can safely aim for much higher utilization than a conventional network designed with large static spare capacity.

---

# 8. But does high utilization mean everything gets equal treatment?

No.

This is another important detail.

B4 uses traffic priorities.

During congestion:

```text
High priority
     ↓
Protected

Low priority
     ↓
Can be throttled / tolerate loss
```

The paper explains that traffic priorities allow high-priority traffic to be protected while lower-priority traffic can tolerate loss or throttling. 

So B4 isn't simply saying:

> "Use every link at 100% and hope everything works."

It's more like:

> **"Use the available capacity aggressively, while protecting the traffic that matters most."**

---

# 9. Another interesting result: load balancing

The paper also examines the ratio between the most and least utilized site-to-site edges.

It reports that:

* For at least **75% of site-to-site edges**, the max:min utilization ratio was **1.05 without failures**
* The ratio was about **2.0 with failures** 

The simple interpretation:

### Without failures

Traffic was distributed relatively evenly.

```text
Edge A → 95%
Edge B → 91%
Edge C → 93%
Edge D → 90%
```

Something like this produces a ratio close to 1.

### With failures

Some paths disappear, so traffic becomes less evenly distributed.

That's expected.
