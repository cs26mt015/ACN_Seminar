# Slide 10 — B4's TE Algorithm

Now we are going one level deeper.

On Slide 9, we learned:

> **B4 needs to calculate how traffic should be split across tunnels.**

For example:

```text
Flow Group A → D

        ┌── Tunnel 1 → 30%
A ──────┤
        └── Tunnel 2 → 70%
```

The question now is:

> **How does B4 calculate those percentages efficiently?**

The paper's answer is:

### **Tunnel Group Generation and Quantization**



---

## 1. First: the ideal mathematical answer

Imagine the TE system calculates that the best allocation is:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 37.3%
Tunnel 2 → 62.7%
```

Mathematically, that might be an excellent solution.

But B4 still has to **implement this allocation in actual network hardware**.

And hardware forwarding mechanisms don't necessarily let you represent arbitrary percentages directly.

So B4 has another step:

```text
Ideal allocation
       ↓
37.3% / 62.7%
       ↓
Quantization
       ↓
Hardware-friendly allocation
```

---

# 2. What is quantization?

For your seminar, think of **quantization as rounding an ideal value into an allowed value**.

Simple example:

Suppose the system can only represent percentages in increments of:

```text
0.5
```

Then instead of an arbitrary value like:

```text
37.3%
```

it may need to choose a nearby supported value.

The paper specifically discusses converting the ideal allocation into **hardware-compatible ratios**. 

So:

> **Optimization finds the ideal answer. Quantization converts it into something the forwarding system can actually implement.**

That's the key idea.

---

# 3. Why does B4 need this?

Because B4 isn't just a mathematical simulation.

Remember:

```text
TE Server
    ↓
SDN Gateway
    ↓
OpenFlow Controller
    ↓
OpenFlow Agent
    ↓
Switch
    ↓
Actual packets
```

The final traffic split must eventually be installed into the switches.

Therefore:

```text
Mathematical solution
        ≠
Automatically usable hardware configuration
```

B4 needs a bridge between the two.

That's one reason the algorithm is important.

---

# 4. Tunnel Group Generation

The algorithm doesn't just calculate one number.

It determines the **set of tunnels and their weights** for a Flow Group.

For example:

```text
Flow Group:
A → D

Available tunnels:

T1: A → B → D
T2: A → C → D
T3: A → E → D
```

The resulting Tunnel Group could look conceptually like:

```text
        Tunnel Group
        ┌───────────────┐
T1      │     50%       │
T2      │     40%       │
T3      │     10%       │
        └───────────────┘
```

The paper gives this kind of example for its resulting allocations. 

So remember:

### FG tells us **what traffic**.

### Tunnels tell us **which paths**.

### TG tells us **how much traffic on each path**.

---

# 5. What happens after the algorithm?

Once the TE Server has calculated the Tunnel Groups:

```text
             TE Server
                 │
        calculates TGs
                 ↓
          Tunnel Groups
                 │
                 ↓
          SDN Gateway
                 │
                 ↓
              OFCs
                 │
                 ↓
          OpenFlow rules
                 │
                 ↓
             Switches
```

Then the switches can actually implement the desired traffic distribution.

---

# 6. How does traffic actually get split?

Suppose:

```text
Tunnel 1 → 30%
Tunnel 2 → 70%
```

B4 needs some mechanism to distribute traffic accordingly.

It uses **hashing** to distribute packets/flows across the available tunnels according to their weights.

Conceptually:

```text
             Packets
                │
                ↓
             Hashing
          ┌─────┴─────┐
          ↓           ↓
       Tunnel 1    Tunnel 2
         30%          70%
```

So you should **not** imagine that a central server is looking at every packet and saying:

> "You go Tunnel 1."

That would obviously be impractical.

Instead, the central system **programs the forwarding behavior**, and the switches perform the forwarding.

---

# 7. The paper's important engineering result

The paper compares its algorithm against the exact optimization approach.

The important result is:

```text
B4 algorithm
     ↓
Similar fairness
     +
~99% bandwidth utilization
     +
~25× faster than LP
```

This is a very strong result for your presentation because it demonstrates that B4 wasn't merely a theoretical architecture.

It was designed to work at production scale.

# 🧠 Professor Q&A

### Q1. What is quantization?

> **Quantization converts an ideal traffic allocation into ratios that can be represented and implemented by the forwarding hardware.**

---

### Q2. Why can't B4 just use the exact mathematical solution?

> Because the forwarding system has implementation constraints. B4 therefore converts the ideal allocation into hardware-compatible ratios.

---

### Q3. Does the TE Server forward every packet?

**No.**

> The TE Server calculates and programs the forwarding behavior. The switches perform the actual packet forwarding.

---

### Q4. How are packets distributed according to the weights?

> B4 uses hashing to distribute traffic across tunnels according to their desired weights.

---

### Q5. What was the advantage of B4's algorithm over the LP?

> It achieved similar fairness while being approximately **25× faster**, with at least **99% bandwidth utilization** in the paper's evaluation.
