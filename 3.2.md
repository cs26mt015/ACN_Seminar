# 4. Very important professor question

Your professor may immediately ask:

### ❓ "Why don't they simply use 100% of the bandwidth?"

This is an important question.

Answer:

> **"Because if a link is already heavily utilized and another link or router fails, the remaining traffic may not fit on the available paths. Keeping spare capacity provides room to absorb failures."**

The paper specifically frames traditional WAN provisioning around masking link or router failures from clients. 

### Simple example

Imagine:

```text
          100 Mbps
A ─────────────────── B

Normal traffic = 40 Mbps
Spare capacity = 60 Mbps
```

Now suppose another path fails and **30 Mbps** of traffic has to move onto this link.

```text
40 Mbps + 30 Mbps
       =
70 Mbps
```

No problem.

But if you normally operated at:

```text
90 Mbps
```

and a failure required another:

```text
30 Mbps
```

you would need:

```text
90 + 30 = 120 Mbps
```

But the link only has:

```text
100 Mbps
```

So traffic would have to be dropped, delayed, or otherwise constrained.

# Slide 3 — Google's Special WAN

Now we're going to answer the most important question:

> **"Why could Google do something different from a normal WAN provider?"**

This is actually one of the **strongest parts of the B4 paper**.

Google had three characteristics that made centralized SDN/traffic engineering practical:

1. Google controlled the **applications, servers and LANs** all the way to the network edge.
2. Many bandwidth-heavy applications were **large data-copy workloads** that benefited from high average bandwidth and could adapt their transmission rate.
3. Google expected only **a few dozen data-center deployments**, making centralized bandwidth control feasible.
